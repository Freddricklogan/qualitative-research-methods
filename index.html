Orientation

What qualitative research is — and when to choose it

Qualitative research seeks to understand how people interpret and make meaning of their experiences, in context and in their own words. It asks how and why, not how many.

Choose qualitative when you need to…

  • Understand a process, experience, or culture from the inside
  • Explore a phenomenon that is poorly understood or newly emerging
  • Surface meaning, context, and complexity that numbers flatten
  • Give voice to participants and honor their interpretations
  • Generate theory or hypotheses grounded in lived data

Philosophical foundations

The four paradigms beneath your method

Every study rests on assumptions about reality (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), values (axiology), and process (methodology). Naming your paradigm is what makes a design coherent rather than a bag of techniques.

Post-positivist

One reality, imperfectly known

Ontology: a real world exists

Epistemology: objective but fallible

Use: structured qual, deductive coding

Constructivist

Multiple realities, co-created

Ontology: reality is socially constructed

Epistemology: meaning made with participants

Use: phenomenology, grounded theory, case study

Transformative

Reality shaped by power

Ontology: historically & politically situated

Epistemology: knowledge serves justice

Use: critical, feminist, participatory, decolonizing

Pragmatic

What works for the question

Ontology: reality is what proves useful

Epistemology: knowledge is practical

Use: mixed methods, applied evaluation

The five approaches

Five ways in — each with its own logic

Creswell's five traditions each answer a different kind of question. Select one to see its focus, data, analysis, and the scholars who shaped it.

Narrative inquiry

Studies the stories individuals tell to make sense of their lives, restorying them into a coherent chronological account. The unit of analysis is the individual and their experience over time.

Best question: "What is the lived story of this person, and what does it reveal?"

Clandinin & ConnellyRiessmanCzarniawska
  • Discipline rootsHumanities, education
  • DataInterviews, journals, letters, field texts
  • Sample1–2 individuals
  • AnalysisRestorying; thematic + structural
  • ProductA chronological narrative of experience

Phenomenology

Describes the essence of a lived experience shared by several people who have all encountered the same phenomenon — reducing individual accounts to "what" was experienced and "how."

Best question: "What is the essence of the experience of ___ for those who live it?"

HusserlMoustakasvan ManenGiorgi
  • Discipline rootsPhilosophy, psychology
  • DataIn-depth interviews (5–25 participants)
  • Key moveEpoché / bracketing of assumptions
  • AnalysisHorizonalization → clusters → essence
  • ProductComposite description of the essence

Grounded theory

Generates a theory of a process or interaction, grounded in and emerging from the data through constant comparison and theoretical sampling — not borrowed from the literature.

Best question: "What theory explains the process of ___?"

Glaser & StraussCorbin & StraussCharmaz
  • Discipline rootsSociology
  • DataInterviews (20–60), to saturation
  • SamplingTheoretical, driven by emerging theory
  • AnalysisOpen → axial → selective coding; memoing
  • ProductA substantive theory / process model

Ethnography

Describes and interprets the shared patterns of culture — values, behaviors, language — of a group in its natural setting, through prolonged immersion and participant observation.

Best question: "How does this culture-sharing group work, from the inside?"

GeertzWolcottSpradleyHammersley & Atkinson
  • Discipline rootsAnthropology, sociology
  • DataFieldwork, observation, artifacts, interviews
  • TimeProlonged engagement (months–years)
  • Key ideaEmic vs. etic; thick description
  • ProductA cultural portrait of the group

Case study

Develops an in-depth analysis of a bounded system — a program, event, or setting — using multiple data sources to illuminate the case within its real-world context.

Best question: "What can we learn from an in-depth look at this bounded case?"

YinStakeMerriam
  • Discipline rootsMultiple (evaluation, education)
  • DataInterviews, documents, observation, artifacts
  • BoundednessDefined by time, place, activity
  • TypesSingle/multiple; intrinsic/instrumental
  • ProductCase description + cross-case themes

The research design workflow

Nine stages from puzzle to defensible study

Qualitative design is iterative, not linear — but these stages give you a spine to return to.

Frame the problem & purpose

Locate a gap; write a purpose statement that signals the approach ("explore," "describe the essence of," "generate a theory of").

Craft the research questions

One central question + a few sub-questions, open and non-directional, aligned to the chosen tradition.

Select the approach & paradigm

Make the philosophical stance explicit; choose among the five traditions to match intent and question.

Design purposeful sampling

Choose a sampling strategy and criteria; plan for saturation or sufficient depth, not statistical representativeness.

Secure access & ethics

IRB approval, informed consent, confidentiality, and a reflexive account of your positionality.

Collect data

Interviews, observation, documents, artifacts — recorded, transcribed, and organized with an audit trail.

Analyze iteratively

Code, categorize, and theme through constant comparison; write analytic memos as insight emerges.

Establish trustworthiness

Triangulate, member-check, keep an audit trail, and write thick description to warrant your claims.

Represent & report

Write findings that stay close to participants' voices, with rich quotation and transparent interpretation.

Framing the study

From problem to research question

A study lives or dies on its central question. The craft is to travel cleanly from a felt problem, to a purpose statement that signals your approach, to a central question that is open, non-directional, and answerable with qualitative data.

The purpose statement — Creswell's script

A single sentence that encodes intent, approach, phenomenon, participants, and site:

"The purpose of this [approach] study is to [understand / describe / explore / generate a theory of] the [central phenomenon] for [participants] at [site]."

The verb is not decoration — describe the essence of signals phenomenology; generate a theory of signals grounded theory; explore the culture of signals ethnography.

The central question — five rules

  • Open — begins with what or how, never whether or does
  • Non-directional — no hypothesized effect or comparison baked in
  • Singular focus — one central question, then 2–5 sub-questions
  • Tradition-aligned — its verb matches the chosen approach
  • Answerable — with words and meaning, not counts or causal proof

Test it: if a number or a yes/no could answer it, it is not yet a qualitative central question.

Worked before & after — sharpening the question by approach

Phenomenology

Before: "Does online learning make first-generation students feel isolated?"

After: "What is the essence of the experience of academic belonging for first-generation students taking fully online courses?"

Why: the before is a closed, yes/no, causal question. The after seeks the shared essence of a lived experience — the phenomenological move.

Grounded theory

Before: "How satisfied are new teachers with their mentoring program?"

After: "What theory explains the process by which novice teachers develop professional identity during their first year?"

Why: satisfaction is a survey construct. The after targets a process unfolding over time — grounded theory's home ground.

Case study

Before: "Is the district's 1:1 laptop rollout effective?"

After: "How did one middle school enact and make sense of a 1:1 laptop initiative during its first implementation year?"

Why: "effective" begs a measurement study. The after bounds a case in time and place and asks for in-depth understanding.

Ethnography

Before: "What percentage of staff follow the school's discipline policy?"

After: "How do teachers and students at one high school collectively construct and enforce the norms of 'respect' in daily practice?"

Why: the after studies shared cultural meaning-in-action, from the inside — the ethnographic aim.

Saturation is the point at which new data stop yielding new codes or insight. It is a claim you must justify with evidence, not assert — document when and how it was reached.
Data collection

Where qualitative data come from

Interviews

The workhorse

Structured · semi-structured · unstructured. Semi-structured — an interview guide with room to follow the participant — is the qualitative default.

Focus groups

Meaning in interaction

6–10 participants; the interaction between them is the data. Strong for norms, shared language, and range of views.

Observation

Seeing practice

Along a continuum from complete observer to complete participant. Recorded as descriptive + reflective field notes.

Documents & artifacts

Traces of the setting

Policies, records, images, and objects — unobtrusive data that contextualize and triangulate interviews.

What makes an interview question good

  • Open-ended — invites description, not yes/no
  • Singular — one idea per question
  • Neutral — not leading or loaded
  • Experiential — "Tell me about a time…"
  • Sequenced — rapport before sensitive topics
  • Probed — "Say more about that."
  • Clear — participant's language, not jargon
  • Aligned — every question serves a research question
Instrument design

Designing the interview guide

A semi-structured guide is a map, not a script. It moves from warm, concrete "grand-tour" invitations to focused "mini-tour" detail, held open by probes that follow the participant wherever the meaning is.

Grand-tour

Broad, experiential openers

Spradley's grand-tour question invites a wide narrative account and lets the participant set the terrain. It is descriptive and non-threatening.

Mini-tour

Zoom into specifics

Once a topic surfaces, mini-tour questions drill into a particular episode, routine, or example — the concrete detail behind the generality.

Probes

Deepen without leading

Neutral prompts — silence, "say more," "what was that like?" — that elicit depth without supplying the answer.

Sample semi-structured guide

Study: the experience of academic belonging for first-generation students in online courses.

  • Opening / rapport"To start, tell me a little about how you came to be a student here." (Builds trust; establishes voice before content.)
  • Grand-tour"Walk me through a typical week in your online courses — what is it like for you?"
  • Grand-tour"Tell me about a moment this term when you felt you really belonged — or really didn't."
  • Mini-tour"You mentioned the discussion boards. Take me back to a specific post — what happened, and how did you respond?"
  • Mini-tour"Describe the last time you reached out to an instructor. What led up to it?"
  • Probes"What was that like for you?" · "Say more about that." · "What do you mean by 'invisible'?" · [deliberate silence]
  • Contrast / meaning"How is belonging online different from what you imagined college would feel like?"
  • Closing"Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?" · "What would you want a first-gen student starting online to know?"
Guide hygiene: one idea per question; keep the participant's words; sequence rapport before sensitive topics; write probes into the guide so you don't improvise leading ones; and pilot it with 1–2 people before the real interviews.

Combining strands

Mixed methods designs

Mixed methods integrate qualitative and quantitative strands to answer a question neither could alone. What distinguishes the core designs is timing (concurrent or sequential), priority, and the point of integration (Creswell & Plano Clark).

Convergent

QUAL + QUAN, then compare

Collect both strands concurrently and with roughly equal priority, analyze separately, then merge to see where they converge, diverge, or expand each other.

Notation: QUAL + QUAN → interpret

Use when: you want corroboration and a fuller picture of the same phenomenon.

Explanatory sequential

QUAN → qual explains

Lead with quantitative results, then use a qualitative follow-up to explain the numbers — the surprises, outliers, and mechanisms behind the statistics.

Notation: QUAN → qual

Use when: you have results that need the "why" behind them.

Exploratory sequential

QUAL → build & test

Begin qualitatively to explore an unknown, use findings to build an instrument or intervention, then test it quantitatively on a larger sample.

Notation: qual → QUAN

Use when: no valid measure exists and you must develop one grounded in context.

DesignTimingPriorityIntegration pointSignature payoff
ConvergentConcurrentOften equalAt interpretation (merge)Corroboration & completeness
Explanatory sequentialQUAN firstUsually quantitativeBetween phases (connect)Explaining the numbers
Exploratory sequentialQUAL firstUsually qualitativeBetween phases (build)Context-grounded instruments
Integration is the point. Running two strands and reporting them in separate chapters is a "parallel" study, not a mixed-methods one. Rigor lives in the joint display or narrative that brings the strands into genuine conversation.
Rigor

Trustworthiness — the qualitative answer to validity

Lincoln & Guba reframed rigor for interpretive work as four criteria. Check the strategies your study uses to gauge how defensible it is.

Credibility

≈ internal validity

Are the findings believable and true to participants? Via triangulation, member checking, prolonged engagement.

Transferability

≈ external validity

Can readers judge applicability to their context? Via thick, rich description.

Dependability

≈ reliability

Is the process consistent and traceable? Via an audit trail and inquiry audit.

Confirmability

≈ objectivity

Are findings grounded in data, not bias? Via reflexivity and confirmability audits.

Ethics & reflexivity

The researcher is the instrument

In qualitative work you are the data-collection instrument — which makes ethics and self-awareness methodological, not just procedural.

Protect participants

  • Informed, voluntary consent
  • Confidentiality & secure data
  • Right to withdraw
  • Minimize harm; anticipate distress

Interrogate yourself

  • Positionality: who you are shapes what you see
  • Insider/outsider dynamics
  • Power between you and participants
  • A reflexive journal across the study

Represent responsibly

  • Stay faithful to participants' meaning
  • Member-check sensitive interpretations
  • Disclose limitations honestly
  • Give voice without appropriating it
CAQDAS tools — NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, and Dedoose — organize, code, and query qualitative data at scale. They manage rigor; they do not replace the analyst's interpretive judgment.
Representation

Writing up qualitative findings

The findings section is where analysis becomes an argument. The craft is to organize around your themes, let participants' voices carry the evidence, and interweave description with interpretation so the reader both sees the data and understands your claim about it.

Structure a findings section

  • Organize by theme, not by question or participant — themes are your findings; number and name them
  • Open each theme with an analytic assertion — tell the reader the claim before the evidence
  • Support with 2–3 well-chosen quotations from different participants, attributed with pseudonyms
  • Follow each quote with interpretation — never let a quotation stand alone as if it explains itself
  • Show range — include confirming and disconfirming instances; report negative cases
  • Save cross-theme synthesis for the discussion, tied back to the literature

Using quotations well

  • Illustrate, don't substitute — a quote is evidence for your point, not the point itself
  • Prefer vivid, representative excerpts over the longest ones; edit ellipses honestly
  • Attribute and contextualize — "Maria, a second-year student, explained…"
  • Balance the chorus — don't let one articulate participant dominate
  • Use short embedded phrases (in-vivo) mid-sentence and reserve block quotes for depth
The description–interpretation balance. Too much raw quotation with no analysis is journalism; too much interpretation with thin evidence is assertion. Wolcott's frame — description, analysis, interpretation — is a useful discipline: show what happened, identify the patterns, then say what it means. Aim for a rhythm of assertion → evidence → interpretation, repeated theme by theme.
Failure modes

Common pitfalls & how to avoid them

Most weak qualitative studies fail in predictable ways. Naming the failure mode is the first step to designing against it.

Pitfall

Thin description

Reporting what was said without the context that makes it meaningful, so readers can't judge transferability.

Avoid it: write thick description — setting, tone, and the meaning behind the act, not just the act.

Pitfall

Cherry-picking quotes

Selecting only quotations that fit the desired story, hiding how typical or rare each view actually was.

Avoid it: report prevalence honestly, seek disconfirming cases, and keep an audit trail of code frequencies.

Pitfall

Going native

Over-identifying with participants until you lose the analytic distance needed to interpret critically.

Avoid it: reflexive journaling, peer debriefing, and scheduled analytic distance from the field.

Pitfall

Analysis-as-summary

Themes that merely restate what participants said ("participants talked about stress") instead of making an analytic claim.

Avoid it: a theme should be a sentence that asserts something — a relationship, tension, or process.

Pitfall

Method–question mismatch

Claiming grounded theory but producing description, or "phenomenology" on a topic no one has lived.

Avoid it: align verb, question, data, and analysis to one tradition — the master table is your checklist.

Pitfall

Ignoring reflexivity

Writing as a neutral observer while your positionality silently shapes access, rapport, and interpretation.

Avoid it: state positionality explicitly and trace how it plausibly shaped what you saw and concluded.

Reference

Core vocabulary

  • Epoché / bracketing — setting aside preconceptions to see a phenomenon freshly.
  • Constant comparison — continually comparing new data to existing codes.
  • Saturation — when new data add no new insight.
  • Thick description — detail rich enough to convey context and meaning.
  • Emic / etic — the insider's vs. the analyst's perspective.
  • Reflexivity — critical self-examination of the researcher's role.
  • Positionality — how identity and stance shape the inquiry.
  • Memoing — writing analytic notes to develop ideas during coding.
  • Horizonalization — treating every statement about a phenomenon as of equal initial worth (phenomenology).
  • Bracketing (epoché) — suspending prior judgments so the phenomenon can appear on its own terms.
  • Axial coding — relating categories to subcategories around conditions, actions, and consequences.
  • Theoretical sampling — selecting new data specifically to develop the emerging theory.
  • Triangulation — using multiple sources, methods, investigators, or theories to corroborate a finding.
  • Member checking — returning interpretations to participants to test accuracy and resonance.
  • Audit trail — a documented, traceable record of every decision from raw data to findings.
  • Negative case analysis — deliberately seeking data that disconfirm the emerging pattern.
